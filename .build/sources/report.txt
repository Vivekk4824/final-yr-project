                                           Abstract
VendorMap is an implemented decision-support web application for managing street-vendor profiles, dis-
covering candidate vending zones, submitting allocation or relocation requests, and reviewing those re-
quests in a municipal officer workspace. The implemented system combines a React 18 progressive web
interface, a FastAPI service layer, SQLite operational persistence, geospatial preprocessing, K-Means en-
vironmental grouping, missing-data-aware multi-criteria decision analysis (MCDA), an advisory mixed-
integer allocation optimizer, and metric validation of small vending slots. Authentication separates ven-
dor and administrator roles and stores session, one-time-password, and phone-identity derivatives rather
than plaintext secrets.
     The active model package contains 308 official zone records, 258 usable analytical references, 146
scoreable free-zone candidates, and a K-Means model trained on 258 rows. The selected two-cluster solu-
tion has a stored silhouette coefficient of 0.3406 and perfect adjusted-Rand stability across five recorded
seeds; these are unsupervised structure measures, not suitability accuracy. A stored MCDA sensitivity
experiment over 224 perturbation scenarios retained the top-ranked zone in every scenario, while or-
dered top-three stability was 56.7 percent. A geographic pilot artifact validates ten 2 m by 2 m slots
for containment and spacing, but explicitly records that field verification and live routing tests were not
performed.
     Final verification focused on features that are complete and suitable for demonstration. Two focused
backend suites completed 97 tests successfully, the frontend suite completed all 58 tests, and the produc-
tion frontend build compiled 1,690 modules without error. Versioned analytical result files provide the
K-Means, MCDA-sensitivity, and pilot-geometry values reported here. Together, these results support
VendorMap as a completed academic decision-support system. The report does not claim field certifica-
tion, legal boundary authority, or live deployment of third-party routing and messaging services.
Keywords: street vending, GIS, MCDA, K-Means, FastAPI, React, allocation planning, decision sup-
port, geospatial validation.

                                       CHAPTER 1

                                   INTRODUCTION

As the opening chapter, this chapter establishes the street-vending allocation problem addressed
by VendorMap. It defines the project’s motivation, scope, measurable objectives, stakeholders,
completed operational workflow, and the boundary between analytical recommendation, ad-
ministrative approval, and legal authority.


1.1 Background and Motivation

Street vending supports livelihoods and provides accessible goods and services, yet the same
activity must coexist with pedestrian movement, traffic safety, public facilities, and land-use
regulation. The Street Vendors Act establishes protection and regulation as joint concerns rather
than treating vending only as an encroachment problem [1]. In practice, a relocation decision
must combine legal eligibility, available capacity, business suitability, access to customers and
transport, and the vendor’s own preferences. These inputs are heterogeneous and frequently
incomplete.
    VendorMap addresses this decision problem as a traceable software workflow. It does not
claim to replace a Town Vending Committee or establish legal boundaries. It provides an au-
thenticated web interface through which a vendor can maintain a profile, inspect ranked candi-
date zones, submit an allocation or relocation request, follow its status, and, for supported pilot
allocations, navigate to a slot. Officers receive a separate workspace for zone status, vendor
records, request review, capacity state, recommendation evidence, and pilot-slot editing. Each
recommendation remains advisory and allocation changes are checked transactionally against
operational state.


1.2 Problem Context

The proposed system addresses three connected technical problems. First, municipal and
OpenStreetMap-derived records must be normalized without representing approximate points
as legal vending polygons. OpenStreetMap is a community-maintained, volunteered geo-
graphic database rather than an authoritative municipal cadastral source; its use in VendorMap
is therefore limited to contextual features and map display [2]. Second, candidate zones
require a reproducible ranking when no verified outcome label such as income or footfall is
available. Third, a selected zone must still satisfy operational constraints: it must be open,
possess capacity, match the application context, and optionally contain a geometrically valid
micro-slot.

    Spreadsheet-only handling makes these relationships difficult to audit. A purely predictive
model would also be poorly justified because the active data package contains environmental
features but no observed business-success target. VendorMap therefore uses K-Means only to
characterize environmental typology, weighted MCDA to make explicit the policy trade-offs,
and a mixed-integer optimizer only as an advisory batch-planning tool. The production path
continues to enforce database checks and human review.


1.3 Aim

The aim is to design and implement a transparent and auditable decision-support platform that as-
sists vendors and municipal officers in explainable zone discovery, allocation-request manage-
ment, relocation planning, and pilot-slot administration while preserving role and data bound-
aries.


1.4 Scope

The system includes a React and Vite client, FastAPI endpoints, SQLite persistence with an
optional PostgreSQL migration path, cookie-based sessions, vendor OTP flows, administrator
login, a multilingual interface, a PWA service worker, live-capacity state, recommendation gen-
eration, allocation requests, officer decisions, audit records, vendor profile photographs, pilot-
slot geometry, navigation-provider integration, and an optional signed WhatsApp Cloud API
webhook.
    The analytical scope covers 308 official zone records. Stored feature-build metadata reports
258 usable analytical references and explicitly records incomplete OSM coverage, missing field
footfall, absence of verified vacancy, and approximate point locations. The active recommenda-
tion candidate snapshot contains 146 scoreable free zones. The scope does not include legal sur-
vey certification, automatic municipal approval, observed income prediction, production load
certification, or confirmed live messaging and routing.


1.5 Objectives

The project objectives are to:
1. maintain authenticated, role-separated vendor and officer workflows;
2. build reproducible spatial features with documented coordinate semantics;
3. rank eligible zones using explainable, missing-data-aware MCDA;
4. expose factor contributions and evidence rather than only a final score;
5. protect capacity and allocation invariants at the database boundary;
6. validate pilot slot containment, dimensions, spacing, and coordinate reference;
7. support multilingual and installable web access without caching private API data;

1.6 Significance and Stakeholders

The primary users are street vendors and municipal officers. Vendors benefit from a single
view of profile data, candidate evidence, applications, and allocations. Officers benefit from
searchable registries, consistent status checks, and auditable review actions. Researchers can
reproduce preprocessing and sensitivity analyses, while system administrators can configure
authentication and optional providers through environment settings. The principal contribution
is integration: spatial evidence, explicit policy weights, operational constraints, and user work-
flows are connected without disguising analytical references as statutory facts.


1.7 Operational Scenario

A typical workflow begins when a street vendor signs in through the vendor portal. An existing
vendor is matched with the protected identity record created during data initialization, while a
new vendor completes a controlled registration form. The profile records the business category,
preferred municipal division, locality preference, language, and practical priorities such as ac-
cess to transport, markets, or parking. These values are used as decision context; they do not
grant an allocation or modify official zone status.
     When the vendor requests suitable locations, the server first selects free-zone records that
satisfy the requested division and verification rules. If live filtering is enabled, zones marked
closed, suspended, or full are removed before scoring. The remaining zones are evaluated
using business compatibility, commercial surroundings, accessibility, nearby facilities, personal
preferences, and historical association evidence. The response shows the score together with
the contribution of each available factor. A warning is displayed when evidence is missing or
when a fallback location is used. This allows the vendor and officer to understand why two
zones receive different positions in the list.
     After selecting a zone, the vendor submits an allocation or relocation request. The request
is stored with a pending status and becomes visible in the officer workspace. The officer can
examine the vendor profile, zone status, recommendation details, current capacity, and previ-
ous activity before making a decision. Approval is not based on the earlier screen state alone.
The backend checks the current request status, business category, capacity, and active alloca-
tion again within the transaction. A successful relocation releases the previous allocation and
updates the destination state. Rejection records the decision without changing occupancy.
     For configured pilot areas, an officer may also assign a specific vending slot. The vendor
can then open the assigned-slot view and request a walking route from the present location. If
the routing provider is unavailable, the destination remains visible and the interface states that

                                          CHAPTER 2

                               LITERATURE REVIEW

Chapter 1 defined the problem, scope, objectives, and evidence boundaries of VendorMap.
This chapter reviews the legal, spatial, and computational foundations of the proposed solution.
It synthesizes work on street-vending governance, GIS-based multi-criteria decision analysis,
AHP weighting, K-Means clustering, allocation optimization, and explainable GeoAI before
identifying the integration gap addressed by the project.


2.1 Street Vending and Governance

Street vending is simultaneously a livelihood system, a distribution channel, and a public-space
governance challenge. Recent spatial studies show that vendor activity forms measurable clus-
ters, but that patterns inferred from street imagery, enforcement records, and resident complaints
need not coincide [3, 4]. Research in Bengaluru similarly links vending opportunities to neigh-
bourhood form, occupational history, and social networks [5]. A 2024 Dhaka study finds des-
ignated space and sidewalk characteristics among the important correlates of vendor sales, rein-
forcing the need to represent infrastructure rather than location alone [6]. The Indian statutory
setting requires protection of livelihood alongside survey, certification, and planned vending
regulation [1]. Accordingly, a software recommendation is not a permit. VendorMap separates
analytical ranking from officer decisions and stores the latter as operational events.


2.2 GIS-Based Multi-Criteria Decision Analysis

GIS-MCDA combines spatial attributes with explicit decision criteria. Recent urban-planning
research uses GIS with multiple-criteria analysis to combine environmental, access, and plan-
ning factors while retaining a visible weighting process [7]. Systematic reviews of algorithmic
planning emphasize stakeholder involvement, auditability, and validation rather than treating a
computed optimum as self-justifying [8, 9]. These findings motivate VendorMap’s factor-level
evidence and weight-sensitivity artifacts. The system uses weighted aggregation, but treats
missing factor evidence by renormalizing over available factors rather than silently converting
missing values to favorable or unfavorable scores.

2.3 AHP and Policy Weighting

The Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) derives weights from pairwise comparisons and pro-
vides a consistency diagnostic [10]. Recent GIS-MCDA applications continue to use AHP for
transparent spatial weighting, while participatory-GIS research warns that technically consis-
tent weights do not substitute for inclusive stakeholder input [7, 9]. If 𝐴 is a positive reciprocal
matrix, its principal eigenvector is normalized to obtain 𝑤. The consistency ratio compares
the matrix consistency index with a random index. VendorMap implements template genera-
tion, expert-review parsing, aggregation, and consistency checks. However, the synthetic AHP
demonstrations are explicitly marked as unsuitable for production use; the active operational
weights must therefore be understood as a configured policy, not as validated stakeholder con-
sensus.

                           SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE

Chapter 2 established the legal, spatial, and computational basis for explainable decision sup-
port. This chapter formalizes the recommendation and allocation problem, then describes the
end-to-end methodology, system components, ranking algorithm, geometry controls, external
integration boundaries, data provenance, and layered decision safeguards.


3.1 Formal Problem Statement

Given a vendor context 𝑣, a set of official zone records 𝑍, analytical feature evidence, live
operational state, and a configured policy, determine an ordered set of eligible zones and support
a human-reviewed allocation process. A zone is eligible only if role-independent rules and
request-specific filters hold; its ranking score is calculated from available evidence. For batch
planning, assign at most one candidate to a demand while respecting zone capacity. The system
must not equate an analytical reference point with an official boundary, a recommendation with
a permit, or a historical association with current occupancy.


3.2 Overall Methodology

The pipeline begins with official-zone and historical evidence, manual reference points, and
cleaned OpenStreetMap layers. Scripts validate identifiers, construct metric-buffer features
in EPSG:32643, prepare training snapshots, compare K-Means configurations, and publish a
versioned model bundle. At request time, the backend resolves the signed-in vendor, applies
zone and live-state filters, calculates factor scores, derives policy and personalization weights,
renormalizes for missing evidence, and returns ranked explanations. Allocation requests are
stored separately from recommendations. Officer approval rechecks capacity and writes the
allocation and audit event within database transactions.
    The completed analytical path is shown in Figure 3.1. It is intentionally one-way: municipal,
vendor, and OpenStreetMap evidence is cleaned before feature engineering; K-Means supplies
an environmental profile, while historical records supply a separate compatibility signal. Only
then does the explainable MCDA stage rank the eligible official zones. Thus, cluster member-
ship is contextual evidence rather than an allocation decision.

3.5 Allocation and Slot Geometry

The optimizer builds feasible vendor-zone edges and solves a mixed-integer model through
SciPy’s optimization facilities. It cannot directly mutate allocations. In the operational work-
flow an officer decision revalidates request state, category context, current capacity, and reloca-
tion effects. Pilot geometry is transformed between WGS84 and UTM zone 43N. Each slot is a
polygon with configured width and depth; validation checks containment, overlap, separation,
and corridor clearance. The stored pilot result is a computational geometry check and explicitly
lacks site verification.


3.6 External Integrations and Failure Boundaries

Optional integrations are deliberately bounded. A routing provider is called server-side; failure
leaves the destination visible without inventing a route. AI-generated explanation support is op-
tional and must recompute eligible evidence rather than accept client-supplied scores. The Meta
WhatsApp Cloud API webhook validates subscription tokens and message signatures, dedupli-
cates inbound identifiers, and shares the conversation service used by the simulator. Provider
configuration and public HTTPS are external deployment tasks.


3.7 Data Lifecycle and Provenance

VendorMap separates analytical data from operational data. Analytical inputs pass through the
numbered scripts in a reproducible order: validation, spatial feature construction, training-data
preparation, K-Means comparison, model fitting, and recommendation export. Each model
version retains input snapshots, derived features, evaluation tables, metadata, and the serialized
estimator. Hashes and generation times identify the inputs used for a result. A new model
version can therefore be evaluated without silently replacing the active configuration.
    Operational data has a different lifecycle. Vendor profiles, live zone status, requests, alloca-
tions, sessions, audit events, conversations, and pilot-slot edits are written through the service
and repository layers. Historical vendor-zone associations are imported as supporting infor-
mation but are not counted as current occupancy. This distinction prevents an old survey re-
lationship from consuming present capacity. Similarly, an analytical geometry is suitable for
computing distances or placing a marker, but it is not treated as a legal boundary unless an
approved source supplies that geometry.
    The API returns projections suited to each role instead of exposing database rows directly.
Vendor responses are restricted to the signed-in identity, whereas officer responses may include
registry and decision-support fields required for review. Sensitive configuration is loaded from
environment settings. Model and data paths are resolved centrally, which reduces differences
between command-line scripts, tests, and the running API.

3.8 Decision Controls

Several controls are applied at different stages of the workflow. Eligibility controls remove can-
didates that cannot be considered under the request. Scoring controls normalize the remaining
inputs and report how much of the configured weight is supported by available data. Workflow
controls prevent duplicate or invalid requests. Authorization controls restrict administrative de-
cisions to an officer session and vendor records to their owner. Transaction controls protect
capacity when two decisions are processed close together. Geometry controls reject overlap-
ping, out-of-bound, or incorrectly referenced pilot slots.
    These controls operate at more than one layer. The interface may disable an action to guide
the user, but the API repeats the validation because browser state can be stale or modified. A
recommendation may show that a zone was suitable at one moment, but approval checks the
current live state because capacity can change after the recommendation was generated. The
optimizer may suggest a batch assignment, but each accepted assignment still passes through
the operational transaction. This layered approach reduces the risk of turning an analytical
suggestion into an unauthorized database change.


3.9 Chapter Summary

The architecture implements a deliberate separation of concerns among spatial evidence, rank-
ing, workflow state, authorization, and persistence. Repeated server-side checks, versioned
provenance, and transactional updates ensure that advisory analytical outputs do not bypass
administrative or operational controls. Chapter 4 next converts this architecture into explicit
functional, non-functional, deployment, and data-authority requirements.

before calculating distances, line densities, fixed-radius counts, and land-use coverage. Stored
metadata lists 250 m and 500 m buffers. It preserves missingness rather than fabricating area
for point references and records fifty missing analytical references.

6.5 Recommendation and Sensitivity Procedure

The operational MCDA loads reference candidates, resolves vendor and request context, com-
putes available factors, applies policy and preference weights, and produces a deterministic
ranking with explanations. The stored sensitivity experiment uses a food vendor request in
Nashik West with no live-status filter. It evaluates one-at-a-time weight changes of 0.10 and
0.20 plus 200 Monte Carlo samples at a 0.20 perturbation bound, for 224 scenarios using seed
42. Its purpose is ranking robustness, not predictive accuracy.


6.6 Pilot Geometry Procedure

Pilot boundaries are read in geographic coordinates and transformed to the local metric CRS.
Rectangular slot polygons are generated from center, heading, width, and depth. The validator
checks all vertices for containment, detects polygon overlaps, measures configured spacing and
pedestrian corridor clearance, and transforms saved output back to EPSG:4326. The stored
result covers ten 2 m by 2 m slots split evenly between two boundaries.

transactional writes so capacity, request status, allocation, and audit state advance together.
The UI consumes these projections through one credentialed client and does not become the
authorization boundary.

6.8 Performance Parameters and Efficiency Considerations

The evaluation combines several measures because no single value captures the quality of the
complete system. Data quality is described by usable and missing-record counts. The K-
Means experiment uses silhouette, Davies–Bouldin, Calinski–Harabasz, inertia, cluster size,
and seed stability. Recommendation behaviour is examined through rank stability and factor
contributions, while slot generation is checked through containment, dimensions, spacing, and
coordinate-reference invariants. Automated backend and frontend tests, the production build,
and browser captures provide software-level evidence.
    Efficiency was considered in the implementation even though a controlled performance
benchmark was outside the available evidence. Analytical features and the fitted clustering
model are versioned and loaded for reuse instead of being rebuilt for every request. The rec-
ommendation service filters ineligible zones before scoring, database operations use bounded
queries and transactions, and the browser receives only the data required for the active view.
Private API responses are deliberately excluded from service-worker caching; this adds a net-
work dependency but avoids retaining authenticated information on a shared device. SQLite is
suitable for the demonstrated academic workload, but concurrent municipal deployment would
require load testing and, if necessary, migration to PostgreSQL.
    No controlled latency, throughput, memory, income-improvement, adoption, or production-
cost measurement was available. Chapter 7 therefore reports only observed test, build, model,
and geometry results, and Chapter 8 labels every monetary value as a planning estimate rather
than a measured project expense.

7.5 Functional Walkthrough

The implemented screens form one continuous user journey. The authentication landing page
first determines whether the user is a vendor or an officer. A vendor session opens a compact
dashboard showing the identity card, current allocation, recent requests, and the next relevant
action. New vendors without an allocation receive a clear entry point to find a vending place.
Existing vendors can review their current location and start a relocation request without losing
the history of the earlier allocation.
     The recommendation explorer presents ranked cards and a map instead of requiring the
user to interpret raw coordinate tables. Each result includes the division, zone type, capacity
context, factor contributions, and cautions associated with incomplete information. A detailed
view supports closer inspection before an application is created. The application area then shows
pending, approved, rejected, or cancelled state. These states are obtained from the server and
are not inferred from local interface actions.
     The officer dashboard provides a city-level overview and separates routine tasks into fo-
cused workspaces. The vendor directory supports text and category filters, pagination, and de-
tailed record inspection. Zone views combine catalog data, live status, historical associations,
and analytical map references. The relocation planning view assists comparison without per-
forming automatic approval. Request review displays the information required for a decision
and submits the decision to the transactional backend.
     Pilot-slot administration extends the zone-level process to a smaller spatial unit. Officers
can inspect a saved layout, select a slot, review its state, and use controlled operations to create,
move, delete, or assign slots in supported areas. Geometry is validated before it is saved. On
the vendor side, an assigned slot becomes the navigation destination. This flow connects the
strategic question of which zone is suitable with the practical question of where the cart or stall
should be positioned.

weight changes. Both observations support showing several candidates with explanations in-
stead of presenting one location as an unquestionable answer.
     The pilot-slot result demonstrates correct coordinate transformation and geometric invari-
ants for the saved test layout. It does not address temporary obstacles, actual cart dimensions,
utility access, crowd movement, road works, or changes in the surveyed area. Those factors

9.1 Summary of Implemented Work

VendorMap provides an end-to-end, role-separated decision-support platform for street vendor
zone discovery and reviewed allocation. The system connects a React PWA, FastAPI routers
and services, operational SQLite persistence, versioned geospatial evidence, descriptive K-
Means clustering, explainable MCDA, advisory allocation optimization, pilot slot geometry,
and optional navigation and messaging adapters. Source organization and screenshots show
distinct vendor and officer workspaces rather than a research script alone.
    The project meets its architectural objective of keeping recommendation, authorization, and
legal interpretation separate. Candidate scores expose contributing factors and evidence cover-
age. Requests remain pending until reviewed. Approval rechecks operational conditions and
creates audit records. Environmental clusters do not become suitability labels. Approximate
map points do not become official boundaries. Optional provider failures do not fabricate re-
sults. These separations are the project’s most important technical contribution.
